## One line
A systematic empirical study measuring how much of a coding agent's performance comes from the harness vs. the model, then dissecting 5 harness components with a controlled modular harness (NanoHarness).

## The problem
Everyone reports end-to-end agent scores, so it's unclear whether wins come from a better model or better scaffolding. On SWE-bench Verified the gap between best and worst harnesses under a fixed model is 19.4 points — nearly as large as the 22.8-point gap between Claude models under a fixed harness. But nobody had isolated *which* harness design choices drive the gains, or whether they transfer across models and tasks.

## How it works
Two studies. First, a factorial evaluation: 2 harnesses (lightweight mini-SWE-agent vs. product-level OpenCode) × 10 models (5 Qwen + 5 DeepSeek) × 3 benchmarks (SWE-bench Pro for issue repair, ProgramBench for repo-level code generation, GitTaskBench for real-world tasks) = 60 configurations. Second, a component dissection: starting from mini-SWE-agent, they add five plug-and-play components one at a time in "building-block" fashion to make NanoHarness — tool registry (structured tool use), context compression, explicit planning, subagents (task-specific vs. generic), and lazy skills — and measure each component's isolated effect plus the combined effect on ProgramBench.

## Key results
Complex harnesses show diminishing marginal returns on SWE-style issue repair as model capability rises, but still help stronger models on more complex, open-ended repo-level tasks. Of the components, structured tool use and **task-specific subagents** give the most stable improvements, while context compression and generic subagents can actually *hurt* repository-generation performance. NanoHarness (the combined variant) improves over mini-SWE-agent by 7.37 points on Qwen3.7-Max and 6.21 points on DeepSeek-V4-Pro, recovering most of the gap to product-level harnesses with only controlled prompt growth. The authors' takeaway: harness gains come less from adding scaffolding or tokens and more from regulating how agents explore — turning raw exploration into structured, task-aligned interaction. (Source: full paper, pages 1–6.)

## How it relates to adaptive decomposition
This paper does no optimization at all — it's pure measurement — so there's no agent-discovered decomposition here. But it's relevant as *prior art for the search space design*: it empirically identifies which harness components are worth evolving (structured tools, task-specific subagents) and which are trap components (generic subagents, context compression hurt). For adaptive decomposition, this is a useful ground truth for what a good initial decomposition looks like and which components are safe to split/evolve independently. Notably, the finding that task-*specific* subagents work while generic ones don't is consistent with the adaptive-decomposition intuition that decomposition granularity should be discovered per-task, not fixed.

## Limits and open questions
Only two harnesses and two model families; OpenCode is treated as a black-box "complex harness" without dissecting *its* internals. Component interactions are only measured as combined-vs-isolated on ProgramBench — they don't map which components synergize vs. conflict pairwise. The study can't tell you *how to find* the right component set automatically; it just tells you where to look. Also, results are SE-only — whether the component ranking transfers to other agent domains is untested.
