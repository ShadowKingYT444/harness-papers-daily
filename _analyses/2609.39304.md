## One line
Automatic harness evolution works for a visual-interface robot agent, but only under two conditions: enough rollouts per round to make promotion decisions trustworthy (B=100 beats B=5, 67% vs 47% held-out) and a Champion–Challenger gate that rejects non-improving revisions — unconditional acceptance drifts downward within ten rounds.

## The problem
A coding agent (Codex) can already control a simulated robot by operating a browser-based 3D point-cloud GUI like a person would use 3D design software (the VIA setup of Hu et al., reaching 96.7% on three LIBERO-Goal tasks). But nearly everything about the agent's performance lives in its hand-written harness: prompt, MCP tool set and semantics, observation format, waypoint control rules, verification and termination logic — a single textual waypoint demo in the prompt can lift one agent from 70% to 100%. The paper asks whether this harness can be improved automatically by another coding agent, and — its real contribution — isolates the two knobs a practitioner controls: the rollout scale behind each promotion decision, and the rule that selects revisions. The difficulty is that for a robot agent each rollout spans tens to 100+ model turns on fresh screenshots and yields one noisy binary outcome, so every promotion decision is a comparison of two sums of noisy bits.

## How it works
Each campaign runs 30 rounds from a hand-written baseline harness H₀. Per round: (1) the task agent (Codex with GPT-5.6 Sol, frozen, 30-min wall-clock cap per rollout) runs B rollouts on a **fixed** training sample of B (task, seed) cases; (2) an optimizer agent (same model, separate persistent session) reads a full evidence manifest — complete trajectory timelines of every case, not summary stats — plus the log of recently rejected challengers, and proposes one bounded commit on top of the champion that must leave the evaluation control plane untouched and pass the repo test suite; (3) a selection rule decides: **unconditional acceptance** (every revision becomes the next base) vs **Champion–Challenger** (promote the challenger only if its success count strictly beats the incumbent's on the same B cases; ties and regressions are rejected and logged). Training uses 10 robosuite/LIBERO tabletop tasks; held-out is 10 different LIBERO tasks, measured once per campaign, never visible to the optimizer. The paper also relates its method to Meta-Harness, Self-Harness, RHI, PRISM, and RHO (which brings harness search to robotics but treats batch size and selection as fixed), and cites the critiques that reported harness-evolution gains often vanish under matched budgets and held-out tasks — the critiques this paper's strict protocol is designed to survive.

## Key results
- **Scale governs trust and generalization:** with 30 rounds fixed and B ∈ {5, 10, 20, 100}, final-champion held-out success is 47%, 54%, 51%, 67% against a 51% baseline. Small batches don't just help less — they overfit: the B=10 champion hit 70% on its training sample but only 54% held-out; the B=5 campaign's every held-out node fell below baseline even as its final champion doubled its training score. B=100 nodes lie above the training-vs-held-out diagonal and move together; small-batch training scores don't rank the nodes (overall correlation 0.60, carried entirely by the B=100 nodes).
- **Scale governs progress rate too:** promotions in 30 rounds were 1, 3, 2, 8 for B=5..100. With 5–10 cases most challengers tie exactly and stall the campaign (B=5 promoted once, at round 3, rejected the other 27); with 100 cases a few-points genuine gain registers as a strictly higher count, so B=100 promoted at rounds 1, 7, 16, 18, 19, 23, 25, 29 and was still improving.
- **Free rein degrades, the gate repairs:** under unconditional acceptance, training success rose briefly then drifted down at every batch size — at B=100 from 42% to 34% within ten rounds, at B=5 from 40% to 0% within 20. With Champion–Challenger, the same loop went monotonically up (41% → 60% training over 30 rounds at B=100, 22 of 30 challengers rejected — most proposed revisions were regressions). Promoted harnesses averaged 54.0% held-out vs 50.9% for rejected ones.
- **Transfer:** a 4-round RoboCasa campaign (10 kitchen tasks, B=100, no held-out) lifted training success 2/100 → 26/100 → 37/100 in two promotions, rejecting two later regressions. The evolved harness transfers to a stronger, unseen model: under GPT-6 Astra the harness gain is worth 46 points (37 → 83), more than under the Sol model it was evolved with — so the gain isn't absorbed by model strength.

## How it relates to adaptive decomposition
This paper is the validation-gate methodology an adaptive-decomposition system desperately needs, but it does **not** implement decomposition: the optimizer edits the whole harness as one flat object with one bounded commit per round — a fixed human-designed decomposition with a search budget of exactly one revision per round. Judge it component-by-component against the user's mechanism:

- **Agent-discovered decomposition:** absent. The optimizer is told to "identify one systemic, task-general failure mechanism" but the component boundaries (prompt vs tools vs control rules) are hand-fixed. It neither discovers nor revises the decomposition.
- **Split/fuse and revert:** absent — and this is where the paper is most relevant as a cautionary instrument. Under unconditional acceptance the harness accumulates ill-judged edits with no way back, drifting down; a recursive split that unconditionally grows the decomposition would suffer exactly this drift. The Champion–Challenger gate is the minimal safeguard for any loop that compounds decisions on noisy binary evidence: promote only on strict improvement over a fixed comparison set, keep the incumbent, and retain rejected challengers for audit (shown back to the optimizer so it doesn't repeat regressive mechanisms).
- **Per-component search budgets:** the paper's real transferable result. If each discovered subproblem gets an independent search budget, the per-decision rollout scale is what decides whether a "split helped" or "fuse helped" verdict is trustworthy. The finding that B=5 promotion decisions are pure noise while B=100 makes them signal is directly a sizing rule for how much evidence a split/fuse decision needs before the decomposition changes. Cheap noisy verdicts plus compounding decisions is precisely the failure mode adaptive decomposition must engineer against.
- **Monotonicity tension:** note a real conflict — Champion–Challenger forces the *champion's training score* to be non-decreasing by construction, but adaptive decomposition's whole point is that local correctness (a subproblem's score) and global correctness (the whole system) can disagree, and the mechanism must be allowed to *revert toward monolithic* — i.e., deliberately accept a locally worse configuration. A naive strictly-improving gate on local subproblem scores would forbid exactly the revert step. So the gate belongs at the global harness level, not per component — the paper's held-out-vs-training-score plot (Figure 2b) is itself the argument: per-sample scores can point the wrong way.

## Limits and open questions
The scaling law stops at B=100 — held-out success was still rising at the largest affordable batch, so saturation is unknown. All experiments are in simulation with rollouts parallelized to ~one episode wall-clock; on a real robot each rollout occupies hardware for its full duration, and the rollout scale the paper demands is exactly what's hardest to obtain there (the authors name physical robots as the next step). The held-out reporting is a single 100-rollout number per node with 5-node means/ranges — no confidence intervals, though the effect sizes are large. The RoboCasa campaign has no held-out set at all, so its 2→37 gain is training-sample only. The strict-beats gate discards ties, which throws away neutral-but-structurally-different harnesses that might unlock later improvements — relevant to any evolution that needs diversity.
