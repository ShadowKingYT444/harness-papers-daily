## One line
HARDE is a two-stage harness-optimization framework for *runtime safety*: it first probes each module of a risk-aware harness (trigger / monitor / feedback) in isolation to distill a natural-language optimization guide, then iteratively optimizes one module at a time against safety+utility feedback — beating hand-designed harnesses and monolithic optimization on three attack benchmarks.

## The problem
LLM agents face runtime safety risks (injected instructions, misleading tool outputs), and system-level defenses split into two inadequate camps: LLM-based monitors that *detect* risk but don't say how detection integrates with *execution-time intervention*, and safety harnesses with predefined rules that can't flex across diverse risks. Building a risk-aware harness means coordinating three coupled decisions — when to invoke monitoring, how to assess risk, how to intervene — but the effect of each module on safety vs benign-task utility is ambiguous, so naive joint optimization can't attribute credit. HARDE's answer: probe modules in isolation first, then optimize with the resulting guide.

## How it works
The **risk-aware harness** augments a base harness with three modules around a monitor model π_monitor: a **trigger module** (screens each proposed action: allow / block / invoke monitor), a **monitor module** (contextual risk assessment of the proposed action), and a **feedback module** (translates risk assessments into sanitized actions or cleaned execution contexts so the agent can continue). **Stage I (probing):** for each module r ∈ {trigger, monitor, feedback}, the proposer generates candidate designs that replace *only that module* of the initial harness, each evaluated on a small probing subset; the proposer then consolidates trajectories and outcomes into a natural-language **optimization guide G** — per-module effects on safety/utility, recurring failure modes, promising revision directions, and a condition–action table mapping observed failures to the module and fix to try. **Stage II (guided optimization):** the proposer iteratively selects the module most in need of revision (using G plus accumulated search experience), replaces only that module, and evaluates the resulting harness on **Safe Success** (task completed *and* no safety violation), averaged over three runs. Final selection uses held-out cases disjoint from probing/optimization. Proposer and executor models are GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.7; monitors tested are DeepSeek-V4-Flash and Qwen3-8B.

## Key results
- HARDE achieves the best Safe Success on all three benchmarks (SHADE-Arena, AgentDyn, Agent-SafetyBench), outperforming unoptimized monitoring, one-shot LLM harness design, and monolithic iterative optimization (Meta-Harness). On SHADE-Arena, the hardest benchmark, it improves Safe Success **77.9% relative to the strongest baseline**.
- It peaks **within 5 iterations** on all three benchmarks — faster and more stable convergence than Meta-Harness, whose comparable search-set performance collapses on held-out tasks (weaker generalization under unconstrained monolithic optimization).
- Ablations show the two ingredients are complementary: modularization alone and guide alone each beat Meta-Harness; together they're best. On SHADE-Arena, removing the trigger module drops Safe Success 0.500→0.357, removing feedback drops it to 0.154 — the modules are non-substitutable.
- **Monitor-aware design matters:** swapping DeepSeek-V4-Flash for Qwen3-8B degrades the vanilla monitor catastrophically (Safe Success −78.3% on AgentDyn), while HARDE re-probed for the weaker monitor stays on top — because the guide is re-derived per monitor capability. Transferring a guide across monitors with very different capabilities fails on hard benchmarks.
- Qualitative analysis (five security properties: task/action alignment, source authorization, data isolation, plus the authors' "epistemic integrity"): modules specialize — trigger gates action alignment and source authorization, monitor covers contextual assessment, feedback restores task alignment and epistemic integrity.

## How it relates to adaptive decomposition
HARDE is the closest existing instance of **per-component harness optimization with isolated credit assignment** — Stage I's module-wise probing is structural credit assignment made explicit: change one module, measure the safety/utility delta, attribute it. The one-module-at-a-time Stage II is coordinate descent over components, and the non-substitutability finding (trigger 0.500→0.357, feedback →0.154) is exactly the kind of per-component importance signal adaptive decomposition would use to allocate search budgets. But the decomposition itself — trigger/monitor/feedback — is **human-designed and frozen**; the agent never discovers, revises, splits, or fuses it. Adaptive decomposition goes one level up: the agent decides *what the components are*. HARDE also shows why that next step is hard: the right decomposition turned out to be *attack-aware* (different attacks stress different safety properties) and *monitor-aware* (guide transfer across monitors fails) — a discovered decomposition would need to re-derive such structure from failure data alone, without the authors' security-property analysis handed to it. The "acceptance without validation" gap flagged in the self-evolving-harness paper applies here too: Stage II picks the earliest harness maximizing search-set Safe Success, with no independent validation gate.

## Limits and open questions
The authors' own Appendix E lists three: (1) only *monitor* models are varied, not agent models — weaker agents often can't complete benchmarks like SHADE-Arena at all, so the results may not transfer down the capability ladder; (2) reliable guide transfer between monitors with substantially different capabilities is unresolved, especially on long-horizon benchmarks; (3) only the external harness is optimized — no joint optimization with agent-model training. Further: the probing subset is small by design (lightweight), so the guide's failure taxonomy inherits small-sample noise; Safe Success conflates safety and utility into one scalar, hiding which one moved; and the three-module decomposition is asserted, not discovered — whether a fourth module (or a different cut) would dominate is untested. Open: can the module structure itself be made evolvable — i.e., does HARDE's probing machinery still work if the proposer is allowed to split the monitor module or fuse trigger+feedback, rather than just revising within the given cut?
