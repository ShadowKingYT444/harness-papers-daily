## One line
A 25,930-episode factorial experiment asking where exactly-once behavior should be enforced — the model, the harness, or the tool contract — when an agent's write times out and retrying could duplicate a charge, post, or deployment.

## The problem
Tool calls cross a network, and networks lose messages: a timed-out write may or may not have taken effect. Blind retry duplicates the side effect; giving up skips required work. The engineering question is *where* the fix belongs. If frontier models reliably verify-before-retry, better models suffice. If harnesses retry transparently or distort what the model sees, the harness is the lever. If some failures are unresolvable by any client-side verification, only the tool contract (idempotency keys) can fix it.

## How it works
The authors build **LIMBO**, a deterministic sandbox of six services with realistic contracts (optional idempotency keys, eventually consistent and missing read paths) and **12 fault modes** injected at the service boundary — including three prior benchmarks omit: late commits (timed-out request still in flight, fixed or heavy-tailed delay), redelivery (at-least-once transport delivers twice), and partial batches. Every episode is graded against a ground-truth ledger of committed effects. The factorial design varies model (9 recent models, 5 providers), harness (minimal function-calling scaffold + 3 production harnesses: GitHub Copilot CLI, Hermes, Codex CLI), tool contract (keys or not), and recovery policy (15 conditions) — 25,930 graded episodes total, exposed via MCP so the same environment runs under every harness. They also prove a theorem: no verification-only policy can be exactly-once under late commits without a known bound on in-flight time.

## Key results
When a read-back can resolve the failure, the **model** decides: frontier models told to act exactly once duplicate a lost-acknowledgement write in only 0.5% of episodes; weaker models duplicate in 18%; the model explains 53% of explained variance. When it can't — request still in flight or transport redelivered — the same frontier models duplicate in 56% and 74% of episodes, and the **contract** explains 81% of variance. Offering idempotency keys on every write drops the duplicate rate from 28% to 4% (agents attach keys in 98% of episodes); with keys plus a guard that attaches them, exactly-once success reaches 99%. The **harness barely matters**: three production harnesses and the minimal scaffold behave almost identically with the same model, and a key-attaching guard transfers across harnesses unchanged. Two sobering numbers: transparent client-side retries cut exactly-once success from 72% to 50%, and in 90% of episodes that produced a duplicate, the agent reported the task as completed. (Source: full paper, pages 1–4.)

## How it relates to adaptive decomposition
No optimization or decomposition in this paper — it's a measurement study of a fixed reliability property. Its relevance is a *negative result about the harness as a search surface*: for exactly-once behavior, the harness is nearly irrelevant and the tool contract dominates. That's a useful boundary condition for any harness optimizer — some failure classes live outside the harness and no amount of harness evolution (adaptive or otherwise) can fix them; the optimizer needs to know when to stop searching and demand a contract change. It also sharpens the "agent discovers the decomposition" story: a genuinely adaptive decomposer should be able to discover that this particular subproblem is *not* decomposable at the harness level and push it down to the contract layer — most current systems, with fixed component taxonomies, cannot express that move.

## Limits and open questions
The sandbox is deterministic and synthetic — real services have messier contracts than LIMBO's six. The "90% of duplicating agents reported success" finding depends on the grading prompt; agents weren't asked to self-audit. The theorem assumes no in-flight bound; real systems sometimes have one (bounded queues), and the paper measures the price of waiting but only under its own delay models. Open: do production harnesses' *transparent* retries (the 72%→50% drop) interact with agent-level retry policies in compounding ways, and can a harness optimizer learn to *disable* its own retry middleware as a "patch"?
