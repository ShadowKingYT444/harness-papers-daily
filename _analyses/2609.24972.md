## What is actually new

RRSI studies a failure mode that is easy to miss: an evolving harness can score higher on the tasks it sees while becoming **larger, more expensive, more benchmark-specific, and worse out of distribution**.

It adds regularization at two points:

- **proposal regularization:** limit and diversify what the optimizer is allowed to change;
- **acceptance regularization:** require gains to beat noise, reject leakage, charge for complexity, and prune weak structure.

The central novelty is not one penalty term. It is treating harness evolution like statistical learning: the highest training score is not necessarily the best harness.

## Mental model

An unconstrained optimizer sees 30 development tasks. It discovers a giant block of special cases that raises the score from 89 to 93. Another candidate only raises it to 90.5 but uses simpler mechanisms that transfer.

If you select by development score alone, you choose the first candidate. RRSI shows this can be the wrong decision.

The method therefore asks three questions about every improvement:

1. Is the measured gain bigger than normal evaluation noise?
2. Does the edit look like a reusable mechanism instead of benchmark leakage?
3. Is the gain large enough to justify the added policy tokens and structure?

## Key objects and state

- **Current harness**
- **Accepted/rejected hypothesis history**
- **Edit budget:** how much may change in one round.
- **Exploration state:** which parts have received little search.
- **Leakage critic:** flags benchmark-specific shortcuts.
- **Noise band:** expected score variation without a meaningful change.
- **Complexity measure:** extra policy tokens / structural burden.
- **Contribution history:** whether an added part has recently helped.
- **Pruning queue:** low-contribution parts that can be removed.

## Optimization loop

~~~mermaid
flowchart TD
    A["Current harness + accepted/rejected history"] --> B["Choose edit target"]
    B --> C["Propose edit under current edit budget"]
    C --> D["Leakage critic"]
    D --> E{"Looks benchmark-specific?"}
    E -- "Yes" --> F["Reject"]
    E -- "No" --> G["Evaluate matched candidate"]
    G --> H["Estimate gain relative to noise"]
    H --> I["Measure extra policy cost / complexity"]
    I --> J{"Gain clears noise AND justifies complexity?"}
    J -- "No" --> F
    J -- "Yes" --> K["Accept candidate"]
    K --> L["Update hypothesis history"]
    L --> M["Prune stale or non-contributing structure"]
    M --> A
~~~

## Step-by-step implementation

1. Start with a seed harness and record repeated baseline evaluations so you know the normal score variance.
2. Give the proposer both accepted and rejected edit histories. A failed idea should not be rediscovered every round.
3. Use a larger edit allowance early. Reduce the edit budget as the harness becomes stronger so late search makes smaller, testable changes.
4. Track which edit areas have been overused. If progress stalls, move some proposal budget to underexplored regions.
5. Before expensive evaluation, run a leakage review. Reject task IDs, answer patterns, evaluator artifacts, or narrow benchmark heuristics.
6. Evaluate the candidate on matched tasks.
7. Compute whether the candidate-minus-parent gain exceeds expected evaluation noise.
8. Measure extra inference cost and structural size.
9. Require more evidence for a larger or more expensive candidate.
10. Periodically test whether recently added parts still contribute. Mark weak parts for pruning.
11. Select the final harness by held-out/OOD performance, not by the highest evolution-set score.

## Experimental setup

The paper reports separate:
- evolution-set performance;
- in-distribution held-out performance;
- out-of-distribution performance;
- policy-token cost.

That separation is essential because the regularized method intentionally gives up some optimization-set score.

## Results that matter

| Method | Evolve | ID held-out | OOD | Policy tokens / trial |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Seed H0 | 89.4 | 86.9 | 39.7 | 1.56M |
| Unregularized RSI | **92.8** | 88.9 | 40.3 | **3.80M** |
| Without proposal regularization | 90.7 | 88.8 | 41.9 | 2.69M |
| Without acceptance regularization | 91.5 | 88.7 | 41.0 | 3.59M |
| **RRSI** | 90.5 | **89.2** | **43.6** | **2.42M** |

This table is the paper in one result.

The unregularized system wins on the evolution set. It loses on OOD and uses far more policy tokens. RRSI accepts a lower "training" score to get the best held-out and OOD result.

## What the ablations mean

**Remove proposal regularization:** OOD falls from 43.6 to 41.9. Search spends more effort in already attractive directions and edits become less disciplined.

**Remove acceptance regularization:** OOD falls further to 41.0 and policy-token cost rises near the unregularized system. This says selection pressure is at least as important as proposal quality.

**Unregularized search:** achieves 92.8 on evolve versus RRSI 90.5, yet only 40.3 OOD versus 43.6. This is direct evidence that development-score maximization can be anti-correlated with the research objective.

## Failure modes and limits

- Leakage critics are themselves imperfect LLM judges.
- Complexity thresholds and schedules still need tuning.
- A simple structural penalty can reject a genuinely useful but complex mechanism.
- "Contribution" is hard to measure when components interact.
- Fixed component categories remain a human prior.
- OOD gains are measured on the paper's selected task distributions; they are not a proof of universal generalization.

## Implementation checklist

Track every candidate on at least four axes:
- task score;
- uncertainty/noise;
- inference cost;
- structural complexity.

Add:
- accepted and rejected edit memory;
- edit-size accounting;
- leakage checks;
- contribution/pruning history;
- an OOD validation set that is never optimized directly.

Do not use one scalar "fitness" unless you can recover all underlying measurements later.

## What RHEvolution should take from it

RRSI is the best warning against uncontrolled recursive splitting.

RHEvolution should regularize **the decomposition graph itself**:
- charge for node count;
- charge for split depth;
- charge for extra interfaces and runtime calls;
- prune owners with no independent positive evidence;
- fuse owners that are always co-activated or co-attributed;
- keep a split only when its OOD gain clears both statistical uncertainty and structural cost.

The important comparison is not "does the recursive graph get bigger?" It is "does adaptive structure buy more OOD performance per unit of search and inference cost?"
