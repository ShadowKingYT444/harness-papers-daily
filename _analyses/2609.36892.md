## One line
Reframes harness self-evolution as a classical learning problem with a three-way risk decomposition — approximation (architecture reachability), generalization (memory scale vs. finite evidence), optimization (biased update dynamics) — and gives each a theorem on a new preference-oriented benchmark, AppWorld-P.

## The problem
Most harness-improvement papers propose a new recipe and report gains; nobody had a shared vocabulary for *why* harnesses hit walls. Three practical tensions recur: (1) mechanism effectiveness depends on what the preference requires — a friendly-tone instruction works as context, but "leave 30 minutes between meetings" needs calendar reasoning; (2) bigger memories can get worse, since accumulated statements interfere; (3) repeated self-evolution plateaus — a correction about a missing signature turns into an unsupported full-name rule plus a spurious politeness rule, and more iterations don't fix it. The paper's move is to cast harness evolution as learning a harness class C around a frozen model, with the risk gap R(π̃n◦f) − R(πfu∗) decomposed exactly as ε_app + ε_gen + ε_opt: the class's reachability (architecture), the finite-interaction estimation burden (scale), and the update algorithm's bias (self-evolution procedure).

## How it works
The formulation: a harness c is a stateful transducer over model history; the induced policy is πc◦f with the model f frozen. For a fixed class C, c∗C minimizes population risk, ĉn minimizes empirical risk from the feedback stream Sn, and c̃n is what the evolution algorithm U actually deploys. The gap between them is the three errors (Figure 2 in the paper maps each to a nested domain: preference → hypothesis → empirical → algorithm). Three empirical+theoretical legs:

**Q1 — architecture as approximation error.** Context harnesses (memory/prompt injection only, āt = at) vs. control harnesses (can transform actions or intervene, e.g., checkers, counters, action rewriting). Theory via a latent-concept model: context only reweights the frozen model's supported concept policies, so Rctx ⊆ Mf (the closed convex hull of prior-supported policies); control transforms traces and can escape it. Theorem 4.1: if the user-optimal policy lies in Rctrl \ Rctx, then ε_app(Cctrl) = 0 while ε_app(Cctx) stays strictly positive — refining context can never close the gap.

**Q2 — scale as generalization error.** Memory-block sweeps at L ∈ {0,1,2,3,5,10,20,60,150} statements. Theory: a stylized model with n feedback observations split across L statements (m = ⌊n/L⌋ per statement), each correct with probability 1/2+γ, gives Theorem 4.2: E[ε_gen] ≤ L·exp(−2γ²⌊n/L⌋), strictly increasing in L for fixed n — a fixed evidence budget spread thinner means more inference mistakes. Coverage saturates as (d−L)⁺/d, so Corollary 4.3 gives a U-shaped excess-risk bound: coverage gains vs. estimation costs.

**Q3 — update algorithm as optimization error.** Four self-evolving recipes (adapted ACE, TEPA, TRACE, Reflexion) run 24 interactions with instance-level complaint feedback that withholds some target details, frozen and evaluated on held-out tasks at checkpoints. Theory: a Markov model of online updates with Wasserstein fixed-point analysis. Theorem 4.4: under contraction (ρ<1), quadratic risk growth (μ>0), and vanishing estimation error, lim inf E[ε_opt] ≥ μβ₀²/(2(1+ρ)²) > 0 — a persistent optimization floor from biased update dynamics that more iterations cannot escape, shared by whole families of update rules.

Empirically everything runs on AppWorld-P (their preference layer over AppWorld: executable per-persona rules on when a preference applies and how compliance is checked from actions/state), foundation model claude-haiku-4-5-20251001, a constrained one-API-call-per-step actuator, and micro-averaged preference violation rate as the metric.

## Key results
- **Q1:** Stating the preference in context drives in-support violations to near zero (private-note format 1.00→0.07, SMS sign-off 1.00→0.00), but leaves out-of-support rates at 0.50 (character checksum), 0.78 (habitual card), 0.44 (running spend total). History-derived context helps habitual-card and running-total (0.22) only partially. Checker rejects (verify + retry, no computed answer) zeros out running totals and gets checksum 0.38 / habitual card 0.39; a harness that actually computes (maintained ledger that rewrites the action's note field) reaches 0.17 / 0.00 / 0.00. Match the mechanism to the preference; context cannot substitute for computation.
- **Q2:** Aggregate violation falls from ~0.77 at L=0 to ~0.20 at L=10, then reverses to ~0.25–0.27 at L=20–150, even though every injected statement concerns a scored preference. The six-rule re-scoring (dropping two possibly-conflicting rules) reproduces the decline-and-rise, so it's not a scoring artifact — more relevant statements past saturation cost more than they buy.
- **Q3:** ACE/TEPA drop from no-memory 0.881 to ~0.4–0.5 by interaction 6 but finish ~0.48, ~0.40 above the oracle's 0.071; TRACE regresses to ~0.57; Reflexion stays near no-memory; full-rewrite (0.689) and fully-specified corrective feedback (0.778) diagnostics still leave large oracle gaps. Repeated updates narrow the gap early, then plateau — and richer feedback alone doesn't close it, implicating the update dynamics rather than just information scarcity.

## How it relates to adaptive decomposition
This paper gives the theoretical vocabulary for *why* adaptive decomposition should beat fixed-architecture evolution — but it does not itself address decomposition at all: the harness is treated as a monolithic policy class C throughout, and all three theorems act on C as a whole. Still, each error term reads naturally as an argument for agent-discovered decomposition:
- **Approximation:** Theorem 4.1's punchline is that a fixed hypothesis class has reachability limits no amount of within-class tuning can fix. Adaptive decomposition attacks exactly this: discovering a new decomposition *expands or shifts* the reachable class (new mechanisms like a ledger-rewriting component move from Rctx into Rctrl territory), rather than optimizing inside a human-set class.
- **Generalization:** the coverage–estimation tradeoff says a component is only worth adding if it carries supported preference coverage. Adaptive decomposition's split/fuse decisions are the operational version of this: split a failing component into finer pieces each with cleaner evidence, fuse working ones to cut the statement budget, and per-component search budgets track exactly what the theorem says matters — evidence per statement, not total evidence.
- **Optimization:** Theorem 4.4's persistent floor comes from biased update dynamics (feedback misattributed into unsupported rules — the signature→full-name example). Per-component updates with local validation are a debiasing mechanism: they confine the update's blast radius so a misread correction corrupts one component instead of the whole harness, and independently-validated edits are precisely what the paper's design advice calls for ("grounded correction signals, independently validated memory edits").
So the paper is best read as the diagnostic framework adaptive decomposition can be argued against: each of its three bottlenecks is one my decomposition moves target, with per-component search budgets and the split/fuse operators as the machinery. But I'd be careful not to over-claim — the authors never decompose C, and their fixed-class theorems don't prove a discovered decomposition reduces ε_app; that's my mapping, not theirs.

## Limits and open questions
The harness is monolithic in the theory — there is no notion of components, so the paper says nothing about when to split or fuse, or whether a finer-grained class actually shrinks the estimation term. Everything empirical is AppWorld-P + claude-haiku-4-5-20251001, one model and one preference-checking paradigm, so the Q1 reachability story may look different with stronger models (a frontier model might compute checksums in-context and collapse the context/control gap). The latent-concept assumption B.1 (context reweights concepts independently of the test task) and the Q2 independence/noise model are stylized; the U-shape bound is conditional on those, not a law. The Q3 oracle gap is partly confounded by design — feedback deliberately withholds the category tag and initials format, so some of the 0.40 gap is information absence rather than update bias, and the paper doesn't separate the two. The memory-sweep degradation (Q2) is under their construction (round-robin statement selection from a fixed pool, no consolidation), so a smarter memory manager might flatten the rise — which is itself the question adaptive decomposition claims to answer. Proofs live in Appendices B.1–B.4, which I couldn't reach from this text extraction, so I'm taking the theorem statements at face value; the assumptions B.3–B.5 behind Theorem 4.4's floor are stated but I haven't verified their proofs. The benchmark is preference-compliance on messaging/payment/playlist tasks — nothing about task-success improvement, which is where most harness-optimizer papers report.
