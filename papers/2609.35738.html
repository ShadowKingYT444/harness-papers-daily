Harness Learning Enables Generalizable Test-Time Adaptation

Day 25 · Oct 24, 2026

Harness Learning Enables Generalizable Test-Time Adaptation

Alvin Zhang, Xuecheng Liu, Zixuan Wang, Fahim Tajwar, Daman Arora, Ruslan Salakhutdinov, Daniel Khashabi, Yuda Song, Andrea Zanette · published 2026-09-28 · arXiv:2609.35738

relevant

One line

Trains a proposer model with RL to learn the harness-revision procedure itself — framing harness edits as meta-learning over executable programs, where revisions play the role of gradient steps — so it can adapt harnesses on brand-new tasks at test time with both models frozen.

The problem

Prompt-based harness optimizers do test-time revision, but each revision is ad hoc: the proposer has no learned notion of how to revise, so revision quality is poor on unseen tasks and requires lots of experimentation. The paper asks whether the revision rule itself can be learned — i.e., meta-learning where the outer loop trains the proposer from revision outcomes and the inner loop applies it to adapt a harness from execution feedback, without any parameter updates at test time.

How it works

  • Inner loop (adaptation): at round t, the proposer sees (task description, current harness ht−1, execution report from running ht−1 on feedback questions), samples G edits, applies them (edits name a code span and its replacement; unparseable ones are invalid), scores candidates on a disjoint scoring question set, and retains the best per the protocol. Next round gets a fresh report.
  • Outer loop (training): only the proposer is updated. Optionally initialized with SFT on successful teacher revisions, then trained with GRPO: each input is a revision context (task + parent harness + execution report), each candidate revision gets reward ri = J(h′i; Qscore) + vi (harness performance + edit-validity auxiliary). Two flavors: Single-step RL (independent revision contexts) and Multistep RL (successive revision states refreshed between phases / generated online, same local per-revision objective).
  • The formal framing: maximize expected held-out performance of the final adapted harness Eθ[J(hT; Qeval)], with training using the immediate revision outcome as surrogate credit. At test time both proposer and solver are frozen.

Key results

  • Reasoning Gym: single-revision scores on 21 unseen families rise from 0.32 (base proposer) to 0.62 after RL; the trained 4B proposer's average proposal beats the 35B teacher's (0.62 vs 0.56) — learning from outcomes can exceed distillation.
  • Multi-hop QA: proposer trained on HotpotQA transfers to MuSiQue and 2WikiMultihopQA; on MuSiQue, Single-step RL averages 0.15 exact match for independent revisions vs 0.27 after ten sequential rounds — and final runs beat even the oracle best of 80 independent proposals, showing sequential compounding matters.
  • Iterative test-time adaptation keeps improving harnesses over multiple rounds; proposers trained only on individual revisions still iterate successfully.
  • Training on revision sequences (Multistep RL) does not consistently beat training on individual revisions.

How it relates to adaptive decomposition

No decomposition anywhere: the adapted object is the harness as one executable program, and revisions are whole-harness code edits selected by scoring. The agent doesn't break the harness into pieces, allocate budgets, or re-decompose. Where it is relevant is the meta-level: it learns the revision rule rather than just the harness, which is the same recursion-depth intuition behind adaptive decomposition (search over the search procedure). But the decomposition axis is orthogonal — one could imagine training a proposer whose revisions are "split this failing component," which this paper doesn't do. It's evidence that learned revision rules transfer across tasks, which supports the plausibility of a meta-learned decomposer.

Limits and open questions

The evaluation is limited to reasoning and multi-hop QA — domains where harness edits are prompt/workflow tweaks, not deep structural code. The meta-learning theory framing (Eq. 5) is aspirational: training uses immediate-revision reward as a surrogate for final-harness performance, and the paper doesn't bound that gap. Multistep RL's failure to beat single-step training is unexplained and suggests the immediate-reward credit assignment may be the bottleneck. I'd probe whether the learned proposer is actually learning general revision skills or mostly learning to be a more reliable edit generator (training's clearest effect is fewer failed/zero-score proposals).
