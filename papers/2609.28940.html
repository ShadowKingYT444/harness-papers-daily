Calibrated Decision Models for Autonomous Penetration-Testing Harnesses: JEV and Laya as System One Decision Layers for LLM-Driven Pentest Agents

Day 29 · Oct 28, 2026

Calibrated Decision Models for Autonomous Penetration-Testing Harnesses: JEV and Laya as System One Decision Layers for LLM-Driven Pentest Agents

Joas Antonio dos Santos Barbosa · published 2026-09-24 · arXiv:2609.28940

context

One line

A position/architecture paper arguing that pentest agent harnesses should replace free-form LLM judgment at key decision points with "System One" models — small, non-generative, probabilistically calibrated classifiers (Jev, Laya) — and reporting one exploratory case study.

The problem

Pentest harnesses use the same generative LLM for both producing findings (recon, exploits) and adjudicating them (is this a real finding? how severe? which agent next?). That coupling causes three pathologies: calibration collapse (confidence doesn't track truth), class-driven severity (SQLi = Critical by default, regardless of demonstrated impact), and compute waste (LLM reasoning spent on surfaces a classifier could dismiss in milliseconds). The author frames this as an assurance gap distinct from the capability gap the field usually measures.

How it works

Four formalized decision points where a System One model replaces generative judgment with a typed, auditable verdict: DP1 finding adjudication (is the finding real?), DP2 severity recalibration (severity from demonstrated impact, not vuln class), DP3 agent pruning (which agents/surfaces to pursue), and DP4 confirmation loops (has the finding been confirmed?). A System One model is defined as a non-autoregressive classifier that evaluates typed questions against a state and returns structured, calibrated verdicts — Kahneman's fast/bounded judgment vs. slow deliberative reasoning. The paper surveys the landscape: TypeSafe's proprietary Jev family (incl. browser-optimized Jev-Ultrafast) and the open-source Laya, reports their published specs without cross-benchmark extrapolation, analyzes training paradigms (RLHF, RLAIF, RLCD, RLHV) for trust implications, and sketches "Rave" — a domain-adapted System One variant fine-tuned on offensive-security decision distributions — as future work. The one empirical datapoint: a single-run comparison of the open-source Rust harness NeuroSploit with vs. without Jev against a 13-vulnerability web target, showing differences in severity distribution, wall-clock time, and data-type-aware grading.

Key results

Mostly architectural/formalization contributions — the paper is explicit (Table I) about what is implemented, measured, or merely proposed. The NeuroSploit case study is a single run with vs. without Jev; the author states plainly it "motivates the architecture but does not constitute a controlled experiment with statistical power." Concrete reported observations (single run, 13-vuln target): differences in severity distribution, wall-clock runtime, and grading by exposed data type. Jev vs. Laya comparison is spec-reported, not measured. Note: this is a single-author independent-researcher paper; the Jev/Laya performance claims are vendor-published specs, not independently verified here. (Source: full paper, pages 1–4.)

How it relates to adaptive decomposition

The decomposition here is fixed and human-designed: four decision points hand-placed by the author into a pentest pipeline. Nobody discovers them; the agent doesn't split, fuse, or re-budget anything. But the underlying design principle resonates: put the right kind of computation at each decision point (fast calibrated classifier for adjudication, slow deliberative model for exploitation) rather than one monolith everywhere. That's a decomposition argument — just a static one. For adaptive decomposition, this paper is a worked example of the kind of component structure an agent could discover on its own, and a caution: the four decision points look suspiciously like the "monolithic fallback" problem in reverse — a human asserting the right granularity a priori. The interesting question the paper doesn't ask: could a harness optimizer learn where System One layers belong?

Limits and open questions

One run, one target, no statistics — the empirical core is anecdotal by the author's own admission. The System One models' specs are taken from vendors; Jev is proprietary and its calibration claims aren't independently tested in this domain. The four decision points are asserted, not derived — is that the right set, and is DP3 (agent pruning) actually safe to delegate to a fast classifier? The sketched Rave model is future work with no training yet. Open: what happens when the System One layer is wrong — a false negative at DP1 drops a real finding silently, and the paper doesn't quantify that failure mode.
