Mid-Harness: Scaling Actions Between Model and Harness for Terminal Agents

Day 61 · Nov 29, 2026

Mid-Harness: Scaling Actions Between Model and Harness for Terminal Agents

Minki Kang, Ryo Hachiuma, Shaokun Zhang, Subhashree Radhakrishnan, Yonggan Fu, Jindong Jiang, Mingjie Liu, Ehsan Hosseini-Asl, Yi Dong, Yu-Chiang Frank Wang, Byung-Kwan Lee · published 2026-09-30 · arXiv:2609.39982

highly relevant

One line

Mid-Harness allocates test-time compute at the model-harness boundary: at each step it samples N candidate actions from the unchanged generator, verifies them with a verifier, and forwards only the chosen action to the unchanged harness — a new "action scaling" axis that complements trajectory-level scaling.

The problem

Terminal agents act through stochastic model generations, and a single bad command (e.g., a wrong package install) changes the environment in ways that doom the whole trajectory — even when the model could have generated a better alternative. Prior work scales test-time compute over trajectories (best-of-N, sequential refinement), but the question of when extra compute before action execution improves trajectory success — and what makes that compute effective — was incompletely understood. In particular, generating more candidates only helps if verification can identify the good ones.

How it works

Mid-Harness is a wrapper around the model call: from interaction history h_t it draws N candidates a_t^i from the fixed generator, runs a verification mechanism Verify_ψ(h_t, A_t) that always returns one of the candidates, and forwards it to the unchanged harness for execution. Three verification mechanisms are compared: listwise (one verifier call sees the whole set and picks), pointwise (independent scalar score per candidate), and pairwise (A/B preference comparisons aggregated by margin-weighted win rates over a ring+pivot tournament; N=8 uses 22–25 comparisons). Under self-verification the same model generates and verifies. To close the capability gap, they distill a frontier verifier (GPT-5.6 Sol): 117k pairwise frontier responses from 732 trajectories over 244 difficult TMAX-15k tasks are used to LoRA-train the TMAX-9B backbone as verifier only, leaving the generator untouched. They also test decision-only (single A/B token) verifiers that skip reasoning generation.

Key results

  • Useful alternatives exist: with a fixed TMAX-9B generator on TerminalBench-Lite, GPT-5.6 Sol verification raises Pass@1 from 50.00% (base agent) to 64.63% at N=4 and 68.03% at N=8 — the generator already produces alternatives that a strong verifier can exploit.
  • Sampling without verification is nearly useless: zero-shot listwise, N=4→8 moves Pass@1 only 49.32%→51.02% and Pass@3 66.33%→67.35%.
  • Mechanism matters: with the generator/verifier fixed at N=8, pairwise reaches 54.76% Pass@1 / 71.43% Pass@3, best of the three.
  • Distillation transfers part of the gap: distilled pairwise verifier at N=8: Pass@1 54.76→57.14%, Pass@3 71.43→75.51%; offline agreement with the teacher rises (pairwise agreement 59.01%→74.58%, verification agreement 38.52%→57.79%).
  • Cost: pairwise is expensive — at N=8, ~26.6k added parallelized output tokens vs ≤1.3k for listwise/pointwise; 125.7k total verifier output tokens. Decision-only variants recover most gains at ~21–24% lower token cost (9B).
  • Composes with trajectory scaling: Best-of-T (T=3) alone: 55.10% Pass@1; + zero-shot/distilled Mid-Harness: 61.22% / 66.33% with the same 3 environment runs. Distilled MH + one SR round: 60.20% vs 55.10% baseline SR. Distilled MH at N=8 matches Best-of-T at T=5 (57.14%) at ~1/3 the reference-priced token cost; MH+SR or MH+Best-of-T(T=3) exceed Best-of-T at T=7 (59.18%) at lower cost.
  • Transfers: gains hold for TMAX 4B→27B, Terminal-Bench 2.1 (21.72%→27.34%), SWE-bench-Verified (46.67%→48.67%), FeatureBench-Mini (1.45%→7.25% for 9B), and across Qwen3.5-9B, Nemotron3.5 Lightning (30B), Nemotron3 Ultra (550B) and the Terminus-2 harness.

How it relates to adaptive decomposition

Mid-Harness treats the harness as a fixed black box and innovates strictly at the model-harness call boundary — it does not decompose the harness into evolvable components, gives subproblems no separate search budgets, and has no split/fuse or revert logic. So its relationship to adaptive decomposition is mostly orthogonal: it's a within-run compute-allocation axis, not a harness-structure optimization. But there are two points of contact. First, the verifier is a functionally separate component that gets optimized independently (LoRA distillation trains the verifier while the generator stays frozen) — a weak, one-sided analog of component-wise search budgets. Second, Mid-Harness is exactly the kind of reusable harness module an adaptive-decomposition loop would want as a component: once the verifier exists as a distinct piece of the harness, an evolution loop could split "verification" off from "generation" and spend separate search budget on it. The paper's core lesson — candidate diversity is useless without a capable selector — also parallels adaptive decomposition's selector problem (revert-when-local-and-global-disagree): both hinge on the quality of the mechanism that picks between alternatives.

Limits and open questions

Distillation leaves a large gap to frontier verification (57.14% vs 68.03% Pass@1); the authors suggest RL-based verifier training as the next step. There are no gold action labels, so verification correctness and the true attainable success under perfect verification can't be measured directly — coverage is probed only indirectly via the frontier verifier. Sampling+verification runs at every action step; adaptive per-step verification (vary N or comparison effort by step difficulty) is left open, as is the comparison against simply spending more reasoning on a single candidate. Evaluation is terminal-agent focused with TMAX models, and every new verifier config needs fresh environment runs (no offline re-evaluation of fixed trajectories). Open: can the verifier be trained on observed command outcomes (world-model style) rather than teacher imitation? And does action scaling survive in non-resettable environments where failed executions have real consequences?
