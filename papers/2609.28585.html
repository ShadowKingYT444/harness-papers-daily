Persistent Billable State: Denial-of-Wallet Attacks and Defenses in Tool-Calling LLM Agents

Day 28 · Oct 27, 2026

Persistent Billable State: Denial-of-Wallet Attacks and Defenses in Tool-Calling LLM Agents

Jinqian Zhang, Haojun Xia, Shujiang Wu, Jingkun Yue, Xia Zhang, Zhangpei Cheng, Bibo Tu · published 2026-09-23 · arXiv:2609.28585

context

One line

A security study of "denial-of-wallet" attacks: a compromised tool return gets retained in agent history and re-billed on every future model call, and the paper formalizes the host-side boundary that should govern what persists.

The problem

Tool admission decides whether a remote tool may be called; nothing governs whether its return may persist as future billed state. A malicious or compromised tool can return schema-valid, task-plausible but padded content; if the host retains it, every later model call reprocesses and re-bills it — one admitted return acquires recurring spending authority. The attacker needs no credentials and no runtime privilege. With full retention, even a 2–10-token trigger can kick off recursive rebilling whose cumulative input grows as O(N²).

How it works

The authors formalize the persistent billable-state boundary: the host's decision over whether and how an external tool return enters later billable context. They derive six denial-of-wallet attack vectors and build DOW-BENCH, an end-to-end harness evaluated across six model families (243 executions), plus controlled history-policy reruns (Full retention vs. Drop vs. Compress) to isolate retention's causal contribution to cost. For defense, they combine deterministic history transformation with four host-side invariants that bound (1) prompt mass, (2) context growth, (3) recursive opportunity, and (4) cumulative spend before reingestion — i.e., the enforcement point is pre-reingestion, at the moment a tool return is about to re-enter billable context. They validate on a 123-evaluation replay corpus and 24 Mistral Small 4 workflows, and scan 3,830 public MCP server/transport repos for existing safeguards.

Key results

Across 243 executions, the maximum per-session cumulative input-token Cost Amplification Factor reaches 14,293× the session's first-call input; 51/69 priced attack sessions cross a $0.10 circuit-breaker, with exposure up to $3.40 vs. a $0.025 benign maximum. Raw retention raises mean effective session cost by 21.2–35.9%. Compression preserves task success on 10/12 and 11/12 history-dependent tasks across two providers, vs. 2/12 under deletion — so dropping history is not a viable defense. The defense kernel "contains" every recurring attack in the 123-evaluation replay corpus; a progress-authorized policy gets 22/24 oracle-verified successes with no pre-completion interruptions vs. 13/24 under a fixed cap. Ecosystem scan: only 71 of 3,830 public MCP repos expose any code-visible safeguard proxy, none covering all four safeguard families. (Source: full paper, pages 1–6.)

How it relates to adaptive decomposition

Not a harness optimizer — no decomposition, no search, no evolution. The connection is through the memory/context layer that adaptive decomposition manipulates: if your mechanism splits components and gives them independent budgets, each component's history policy becomes part of its fitness surface, and this paper shows retention policy has both a cost dimension (up to 35.9% session-cost inflation from raw retention) and a security dimension (attacker-controlled persistence). A decomposition scheme that evolves components in parallel without governing what each component retains is building on a compromised foundation. The "four invariants" framing is also a useful design pattern for adaptive decomposition's own budgets: bound growth before reingestion rather than after the bill arrives.

Limits and open questions

The defense is host-side and deterministic — it doesn't cover semantic attacks where the payload is genuinely task-relevant but subtly malicious (the paper positions this as complementary to instruction-takeover defenses). The six attack vectors are derived analytically; attacker adaptation against the four invariants isn't evaluated. The ecosystem scan only counts code-visible proxies in public repos, which undercounts private deployments. Open: how do the invariants interact with legitimate long-horizon workflows that need large persistent state — is the progress-authorized policy's 22/24 success rate the ceiling, and what breaks in the other 2?
